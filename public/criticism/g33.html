A region defined by movement as well as rule

The publisher's contents outline moves from prehistory, food and rivers to textual traditions, political formations, inscriptions, cross-border continuity and modern migration. That scope promises a regional history whose organising space exceeds a sequence of dynasties. The inclusion of a chapter about disciplined reading of tradition is especially significant: narrative inheritance and historical evidence require a stated relationship.

This is a critical introduction to the advertised structure, not a review of the full book. Its central evidentiary test would be how archaeological, textual and later social materials are compared without being made to say the same thing. A listed hypothesis about two Vidyapatis, for example, must be evaluated through its argument and sources; its presence in a contents list does not establish it as settled history. The outline makes that further reading worthwhile, but cannot replace it.

A QUESTION TO CARRY BACK

How does the book mark the difference between a remembered tradition, a hypothesis and an independently supported historical claim?
