History moves into the household and workplace

The publisher's contents foreground subsistence, households, women's labour, crafts, exchange, health and religious life. That organisation promises to widen regional history beyond rulers and political transitions. It asks what people did to sustain life and how social arrangements shaped that work.

The promise also creates a demanding evidentiary task. A household practice, a prescriptive text and a land grant do not offer the same kind of evidence; neither does one period automatically explain another. A full review would need to follow how the book makes those distinctions, particularly when discussing groups unevenly represented in surviving records. This note is a critical introduction to the publisher's outline, not confirmation of the book's historical conclusions. The value of the proposed scope will depend on the care of its local demonstrations.

Whose labour becomes visible in a particular source, and whose remains present only through inference?
