Ganapati invocation — Editorial

Ashish Anchinhaar

Anchinhaar explains Setushama’s bridge metaphor through a Samavedic hymn and links it to ethical obstacles in literary life. He describes the second volume as a complement to the first, commissioned mainly from different contributors and composed of new articles. Seven invocations structure its presentation. The preface acknowledges subjects still uncovered and welcomes corrections; its Vedic interpretation and account of the literary field remain the editor’s framing.

A Proficient Writer of the Child’s Mind — Mrs. Preeti Thakur

Munni Kamat

Kamat argues that children’s writing requires simplicity of feeling and an imaginative return to the child’s perspective. Surveying four books, she highlights their illustrations and cultural transmission. Her most concrete example is the Purusha Pariksha activity book, where children identify stories, supply titles and dialogue, and colour drawings—combining literary inheritance with active participation.

Preeti Thakur’s Contribution to Maithili Picture Stories

Ira Mallick

Mallick situates picture narrative between oral storytelling, visual art and children’s first encounters with language. She surveys translations and original collections, praises the use of line, colour and Mithila painting techniques, and emphasizes accessibility before alphabetic literacy. The essay interprets the books as a connection between younger readers and regional culture; its broad claims are attributed to Mallick.

The Creator of the Digital Era for Maithili: Gajendra Thakur

Kailash Kumar Thakur

Thakur surveys digital publishing, Wikipedia, dictionaries, manuscript access and multiple scripts, then argues that textual resources alone cannot secure Maithili’s future. He proposes podcasts, oral histories, video essays and subtitled scholarly conversations. The appended editorial response welcomes the challenge but points to existing audiovisual work, limited unpaid resources and the need for independent teams to share responsibility.

Maithili in the Digital Age: Videha and Gajendra Thakur

Vikas Vatsnabh

Vatsnabh connects language survival online with usable scripts, Unicode, technical vocabulary and substantial searchable content. He treats Videha as a journal, library and resource aggregator, and discusses dictionaries and Panji publication as collaborative work. The closing argument looks toward AI: sustained digital availability, rather than symbolic recognition alone, is presented as essential infrastructure for Maithili.

Gajendra Thakur’s Contribution to the Preservation of Mithila’s Panji

Bhairav Lal Das

Das separates Panji’s value as a historical source from the hierarchy and misuse associated with its social administration. He credits the joint editors of the published records and recounts practical conservation efforts frustrated by mistrust, declining livelihoods and deteriorating manuscripts. He calls for further preservation and publication while considering how migration and changing marriage practices alter the records’ former function.

Diversity and Videha: The Consciousness-Based Renaissance of Modern Maithili Literature

Poonam Jha ‘Sudha’

Jha organizes her argument around linguistic, writerly and thematic diversity. She presents Videha’s acceptance of regional speech, younger voices and varied subjects as a challenge to narrow standards and centralized literary authority. Her account treats digital access as a bridge between rural and urban readers, generations and the diaspora, while discussing resistance to this openness as part of the debate.

Videha and New Writers

Kalpana Jha

Jha defines editing as mentorship rather than selection alone. Drawing on her own beginnings and the experiences of other writers, she describes encouragement, suggested reading, criticism and help with genre and craft. Her central demand is that guidance strengthen a writer’s distinct voice while building a new generation of contributors and connecting their abilities with readers’ expectations.

Scarcity of Non-Forward Caste Writers in Maithili and Gajendra Thakur

Munnaji

Munnaji disputes the claim that Maithili lacks writers from socially marginalized communities. He argues that publication opportunities and sustained encouragement make previously excluded talent visible, using Jagadish Prasad Mandal and later contributors as examples. The essay presents Videha’s significance as the creation of an enduring alternative stream; its account of literary gatekeeping is the contributor’s polemical interpretation.

The Pro-People Stance of Videha Magazine

Dr. Dhanakar Thakur

Thakur evaluates Videha through five areas: language, writers, subjects and genres, awards, and special issues. He values openness beyond a single ideology, while acknowledging uneven spelling and disagreeing with the naming of parallel awards. The resulting assessment is supportive but qualified: widening participation matters, yet a magazine’s public responsibility remains unfinished and open to criticism.

Gajendra Thakur and the Discipline of Maithili Criticism

Hitnath Jha

Jha reviews Maithili Samikshashastra as a guide to evaluating genres rather than praising personalities. He discusses distinctions between short narrative forms, the demands of ghazal metre, other poetic forms, translation and children’s literature. The review emphasizes judging craft and meaning without imposing the critic’s ideology, and presents the book as useful to writers, teachers, students and researchers.

Gajendra Thakur: The Bridge-Person for the Renaissance of Maithili Language and Literature

Ujjwal Kumar Jha

Jha uses bridge-building to connect Thakur’s writing, historical research, script preservation, dictionaries, translation and editorial work. He interprets literary creation as a cultural responsibility extending to young readers and future researchers. The essay’s organizing idea is continuity across tradition and technology, reinforced by an account of translation as exchange between Maithili, English and other Indian languages.

Gajendra Thakur’s Absence from the Forums of Mithila–Maithili: Causes and Effects

Mukesh Anand

Anand contrasts public-platform recognition with sustained work away from ceremonies, drawing on his experience of Delhi’s literary environment. He offers possible explanations for Videha’s limited institutional visibility and asks readers to judge contribution through study rather than invitations or awards. He also stresses collaborators’ roles and argues that distance from platforms may preserve time for substantive work.
